The history of Lisbon, the capital of Portugal, stretches back more than three thousand years and is one of the oldest cities in Western Europe. Archaeological evidence shows that Phoenician traders settled around the Tagus estuary as early as 1200 BCE, calling the place Alis Ubbo, and later the Greeks, Carthaginians and Romans valued its sheltered harbour. Under Roman rule the city was known as Olisipo and grew into an important port of the province of Lusitania, with temples, baths and a forum built on the hills above the river. After the fall of the Roman Empire the region passed through the hands of Germanic tribes, then Alans and Suebi, and from 711 CE it was part of the Umayyad and later Almoravid domains of al-Andalus, when the city was called al-Ushbuna and fortified by a Moorish citadel. In 1147 the first king of Portugal, Afonso Henriques, captured the city with the help of a crusader fleet bound for the Holy Land, and Lisbon became the royal capital in 1255, a position it has held ever since. In the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries the city was the launching point of Portuguese maritime exploration, and the wealth of the spice trade from India, Africa and Brazil paid for the Belém Tower, the Jerónimos Monastery and the palaces of the Age of Discovery. On the morning of 1 November 1755 a massive earthquake, followed by a tsunami and fires, destroyed most of the medieval city and killed tens of thousands of people, an event that shocked Enlightenment Europe. The prime minister, the Marquis of Pombal, rebuilt the ruined centre on a rational grid of wide streets and earthquake resistant buildings, creating the Baixa district that still defines the city today. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries Lisbon expanded along the river and the hills, absorbed waves of refugees and migrants, and in 1986 Portugal joined the European Communities, bringing new growth and investment to the capital. Today Lisbon is a city of nearly six hundred thousand inhabitants in its historic core, with a metropolitan area of more than two and a half million people, mixing Roman walls, Moorish alleys, Manueline monuments, Pombaline squares, tiled facades, fado music and a busy modern port, and it is one of the most visited capitals of southern Europe.
